\begin{abstract}
\noindent Hyperliquid, Binance and Bybit set perpetual funding by one rule: while the averaged premium lies in a 10~bp band, funding equals an interest rate of $0.01\%$ per eight hours, and a settlement reveals the premium only as that band. From 2~October 2025 to 28~April 2026, $46.0\%$ of Hyperliquid's hourly BTC settlements sit at that rate, against $5.9\%$ on Binance and $7.7\%$ on Bybit. From 11~December 2023 to 30~September 2026 the shares are $55.2\%$, $23.8\%$ and $27.3\%$ for BTC and $52.0\%$, $23.1\%$ and $28.3\%$ for ETH; taken together, the two quarters in which the exchanges' premiums were positive reverse the ordering. Matched hour by hour with the exchanges' own premium indices, Hyperliquid's BTC premium lies a median 2.2~bps above both (ETH 1.7), in four hours out of five and in every quarter. For BTC the gap persists but varies: 3.8~bps in 2024, 3.0 to 3.1 in 2025, 0.3 to 0.5 in 2026Q1 and 2026Q2 and 1.3 to 1.5 in 2026Q3. By a Shapley split it accounts for about all of the difference in shares from 2024Q3 to 2025Q4, but for less than half in 2026Q1 and 2026Q2 (ETH: $55\%$ to $70\%$), when all three median premiums lay below the band and dispersion mattered more. Cross-venue comparisons of positioning should therefore use premiums, with their constructions in view, not headline rates. A two-state Markov-switching AR(1) on the premium reverts faster under stress only around October 2025: allowing for switching, the half-life ratio is 1.5, not significantly different from one under a misspecification-robust covariance that one hour of the 10~October crash dominates, and later data reverse it. A three-state Gaussian hidden Markov model separates hourly volatility into $14.6\%$, $33.3\%$ and $88.4\%$ annualised states and estimates the direct move from the high to the low state at zero to three decimals, as do its fits to simulated single-regime Student-$t$ GARCH paths. All results are descriptive.

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Perpetual futures are the most traded cryptocurrency derivatives \citep{he2022perp}, and decentralised venues, Hyperliquid foremost \citep{lau2026carry}, now trade them at scale. Having no expiry, a perpetual is tied to its index by a recurring funding payment between longs and shorts, set from the contract's averaged premium over its index \citep{ackerer2024,hyperliquid_funding_docs}. Hyperliquid, Binance and Bybit compute that payment with one rule, which pays exactly an interest rate whenever the averaged premium lies inside a band 10~bps wide. Inside the band the published rate stops moving with the market. How often a venue's rate sits there decides whether its headline funding can be read as a signal of positioning, and whether funding compared across venues compares like with like.

Funding has a literature on carry, basis and, increasingly, the rule itself. \citet{schmeling2025crypto} trace the large and volatile basis of crypto futures to trend-chasing demand for leverage meeting limited arbitrage capital, \citet{makarov2020trading} document the frictions that segment crypto markets, and \citet{alexander2020bitmex} study price discovery on the first large perpetual venue. \citet{werapun2026funding}, \citet{chen2024perpetual} and \citet{erez2026venues} compare funding, its mechanisms and its arbitrage across centralised and decentralised venues, \citet{erez2026timing} show how the timing of funding shapes no-arbitrage bounds on two decentralised venues, and \citet{lau2026carry} finds Hyperliquid's funding about $7\%$ a year above Binance's and Bybit's over three years. \citet{zhivkov2026funding,zhivkov2026temporal} correlate and Granger-test the funding rates of 26 centralised and decentralised venues, Hyperliquid, Binance and Bybit among them, between November 2025 and January 2026, and find funding less correlated between decentralised venues and the exchanges than among the exchanges, a two-tier market in their reading. Pricing models treat the clamp explicitly \citep{hugonnier2025clamp,he2022perp}, \citet{kim2025funding} derive funding rates that keep a perpetual's price anchored to its target, also when the rate is averaged over the past eight hours, and \citet{hewangzhang2026} argue that averaging and the rule's flat and capped regions separate the nominal funding template from the funding delivered at settlement. Counting settlements at the rule's flat value has precedents: \citet{he2022perp} find $41.7\%$ of Binance's BTC settlements at the interest rate over 2020--2024, \citet{ahmed2026event} report a mass at the formula point on centralised exchanges but not on dYdX, whose funding carries no interest component, and in a companion study over February 2025 to February 2026 find Hyperliquid's funding distribution clustering with the exchanges' rather than with dYdX's \citep{ahmed2026interest}; \citet{lim2026rarely,lim2026inaction} counts $73\%$ of Kalshi's settlements exactly at zero, with Bybit's BTC premium, at a mean of about $-4.7$~bps over five weeks, as the offshore comparison. On the regime side, hidden Markov and Markov-switching models of Bitcoin returns \citep{giudici2020,koki2022bitcoin,machimbo2025,ardia2019} build on \citet{hamilton1989} and \citet{rabiner1989}.

We make three contributions, all descriptive. First, we measure the censoring exactly, from the venues' decimal settlements, for a decentralised venue and two exchanges over common windows: the 2~October 2025 to 28~April 2026 window of our regime models, and the whole period since Hyperliquid adopted the exchanges' clamp, 11~December 2023 to 30~September 2026, for BTC and ETH (Section~\ref{sec:censoring}). Hyperliquid is censored far more often than either exchange in most months, but not in all: its share fell below both exchanges' in four months for BTC and three for ETH, mostly in 2024, in each of which $41\%$ to $75\%$ of its hours lay above the band; taken together, the two quarters in which the exchanges' premiums were positive reverse the ordering. Second, we measure the premium gap behind that ordering, hour by hour and with each venue's own premium construction, and split the difference in censored shares into the gap's location, the averaging window and the premiums' dispersion (Section~\ref{sec:gap}). Third, we describe the regime dynamics of Hyperliquid's premium, with a misspecification-robust (sandwich) covariance beside the Hessian and switching-adjusted half-lives, and of its returns, and report two cautions that travel: an asymmetry in mean reversion that belongs to one episode, estimated on a likelihood whose most frequent optimum is not its maximum, and a boundary zero in a hidden Markov kernel that a single-regime GARCH process produces as well (Section~\ref{sec:regimes}).

Section~\ref{sec:rule} sets out the rule on the three venues and Section~\ref{sec:data} the data; Section~\ref{sec:implications} draws the implications and states the limitations, and Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes.

\section{The funding rule on three venues}\label{sec:rule}

Hyperliquid's documentation gives the eight-hour funding rate as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:rule}
  F_t \;=\; \bar P_t + \operatorname{clamp}\bigl(r - \bar P_t,\, -c,\, +c\bigr),
\end{equation}
with $\bar P_t$ the premium index averaged over the funding interval, $r = 0.01\%$ per eight hours the interest rate, $c = 0.05\%$ the clamp and $\operatorname{clamp}(x,a,b) = \max(a,\min(b,x))$; Hyperliquid pays $F_t/8$ every hour \citep{hyperliquid_funding_docs}. Binance and Bybit apply the same $r$ and $c$ and settle every eight hours \citep{binance_funding_docs,bybit_funding_docs}. The fixed $0.01\%$ is an industry convention \citep{amirov2026anchor}, and Hyperliquid states that it adopts it for consistency with the exchanges.

The clamp is the identity when $|r - \bar P_t| \le c$, that is, when $\bar P_t$ lies in the band $[r-c,\, r+c] = [-4, +6]$~bps, centred on $+1$~bp. There $F_t = r$ exactly: the rule pins the rate at the interest rate. Below the band the rate is $\bar P_t + c$, below $r$; above it, $\bar P_t - c$, above $r$. A settlement outside the band therefore identifies the averaged premium exactly, while one at $r$ reveals only that the premium lay in the band. As a measurement of the premium the rate is interval-censored, a case of middle censoring \citep{jammalamadaka2003} or coarse data \citep{heitjan1991}. We call such settlements censored and, as a label only, call $r$ the floor: the rate falls below it whenever the premium lies below the band, as it does in most exchange settlements (Section~\ref{sec:censoring}).

Hyperliquid has not always used this rule. Its published rates, classified record by record, settle every eight hours until 8~June 2023 and hourly since. Until 11~December 2023 they fit a $\pm 0.03\%$ clamp (a band of $-2$ to $+4$~bps), with two exceptions: from 16~June to 15~July 2023 they equal the premium alone, and a few records on 16~July 2023 fit a $\pm 0.02\%$ clamp. From the record stamped 11~December 2023 23:00~UTC they fit the exchanges' $\pm 0.05\%$ (Table~\ref{tab:construction}, note a). These rules and dates are inferred from the data; we found no announcement. Under the common rule a Hyperliquid hour is at the floor exactly when its premium lies in the band, with no exception in $24{,}577$ hours for BTC or ETH. Every long-run comparison below starts at the record of 11~December 2023 23:00~UTC.

The rule is common; the premium that enters it is not (Table~\ref{tab:construction}), a point \citet{erez2026venues} make for perpetual venues in general. Hyperliquid averages premiums sampled every five seconds over one hour; Binance and Bybit average over eight hours with weights rising linearly towards settlement, Bybit with equal weights until 7~March 2024, a switch we find in its settlements. Hyperliquid's reference price is a stake-weighted median of its validators' weighted medians of spot mid prices on seven exchanges (Table~\ref{tab:construction}, note~b); the exchanges use weighted-average indices, in which Binance's spot book has the largest BTCUSDT weight on both exchanges (note~d). Impact notionals differ, and Hyperliquid margins its USDT-denominated contracts in USDC without conversion. Each of these could shift the averaged premium; we do not identify which does.

\section{Data}\label{sec:data}

\begin{sloppypar}\noindent Hyperliquid's two public endpoints are \texttt{fundingHistory}, which returns each settlement (eight-hourly until 8~June 2023, hourly since) with the averaged premium it was computed from, for BTC and ETH from 12~May 2023 to the settlement stamped 1~October 2026 00:00~UTC, which pays for the last hour of 30~September; and \texttt{candleSnapshot}, which returns candles of traded prices. For Binance and Bybit we take the BTCUSDT and ETHUSDT perpetuals' funding settlements and their one-minute premium-index klines, which we aggregate to the hours Hyperliquid's records cover (Appendix~\ref{app:gap}). Rates are compared as decimals parsed from the venues' strings, so `at the floor' is an exact comparison. Figure~\ref{fig:windows} maps the windows and the series.\end{sloppypar}

The regime models use the estimation window, 2~October 2025 00:00 to 28~April 2026 08:00~UTC: $5{,}001$ hourly Hyperliquid settlements and $626$ settlements per exchange. The later window runs to 30~September 2026 ($3{,}736$ hours, $467$ settlements) and the long history from 11~December 2023 23:00 ($24{,}577$ hours, $3{,}073$ settlements per exchange). Hyperliquid publishes no record stamped 15~August 2024 13:00~UTC for either asset, so the long history holds one hour fewer than it spans, and the block comparison of Section~\ref{ssec:history} uses the $3{,}071$ settlement blocks with all eight hourly records under the common rule: the first settlement, 12~December 2023 00:00, averages six hours from before the switch, and the block ending 15~August 2024 16:00 lacks that hour (the Shapley split averages Hyperliquid's hours in all $3{,}073$ blocks). Funding is otherwise fully replicable, because \texttt{fundingHistory} keeps a contract's whole history; the premium series re-retrieved in August and October 2026 equals the archived panel digit for digit on all $3{,}507$ overlapping hours. Hourly prices are not: \texttt{candleSnapshot} serves only the latest $5{,}000$ or so candles at each interval, so the hours before 3~December 2025 had left its hourly window before the panel was archived. The return fit on the whole estimation window (Section~\ref{ssec:hmm}) rests on the original extract, retrieved on 28~April 2026; every other result is rebuilt from shipped data, the return-side ones on the hourly sub-period, 3~December 2025 to 28~April 2026 ($3{,}507$ hours). Two-hourly candles cover the whole estimation window and agree with the surviving hourly panel aggregated to two hours on all $1{,}753$ overlapping bars.

Table~\ref{tab:summary} summarises the BTC series. Over the estimation window Hyperliquid's BTC perpetual traded between hourly closes of \$126{,}101 (6~October 2025) and \$62{,}883 (24~February 2026), as the original extract records them. The premium averaged $-3.15$~bps, the contract trading below its index for most of the window, while funding averaged $+0.059$~bps per hour, about $5.2\%$ a year, longs paying shorts: in the $46.0\%$ of hours with the premium in the band the contract paid the floor whatever its positioning.

Figure~\ref{fig:censoring}(a) draws the rule: funding is flat at the floor across the band and moves one for one with the premium outside it. Over the estimation window Binance prints the floor in $37$ of $626$ settlements ($5.9\%$) and Bybit in $48$ ($7.7\%$), against $2{,}300$ of $5{,}001$ hours ($46.0\%$) on Hyperliquid (Table~\ref{tab:censoring}). No exchange settlement lies above the floor, and $94.1\%$ of Binance's and $92.3\%$ of Bybit's lie below it. The five months after the window repeat the pattern: $51.7\%$ on Hyperliquid against $10.9\%$ and $7.9\%$.

The premiums explain the difference (Figure~\ref{fig:censoring}(b)). Recovered exactly from the settlements outside the band, the exchanges' premiums are packed just below the band's lower edge, with medians of $-4.75$ and $-4.82$~bps and interquartile ranges of $0.66$ and $0.60$~bps. Hyperliquid's straddles the edge: its median is $-4.11$~bps, $52.5\%$ of its hours lie below the band, and a long tail into the band lifts its mean to $-3.15$.

The hourly schedule does not cause the difference. Read every eighth hour, Hyperliquid's series is at the floor in $44.2\%$ to $48.4\%$ of hours depending on the phase, and in $48.4\%$ at the exchanges' 00, 08 and 16~UTC stamps; averaged over the eight hours each exchange settlement pays for, with the exchanges' weights rising towards settlement, its premium lies in the band $46.5\%$ of the time ($48.6\%$ with equal weights), against $46.0\%$ of hours. An hourly schedule multiplies censored payments, not the censored share.

\section{The premium gap}\label{sec:gap}

\subsection{Measurement}

Since all three venues apply the same band, which of them prints the floor depends only on where its averaged premium sits. We therefore compare the premiums directly, at Hyperliquid's resolution. For each hour we average the closes of Binance's and Bybit's one-minute premium-index klines over the hour Hyperliquid's record averages and define the gap $D_t = P^{\text{HL}}_t - P^{X}_t$ in eight-hour-equivalent bps, $X$ being the exchange (Appendix~\ref{app:gap}). The construction reproduces the exchanges' own figures. For BTC, the close of the last minute of each hour equals the close of the exchange's own hourly premium-index kline, shipped in \texttt{data/interim}, in all $5{,}001$ hours of the estimation window; and from 11~December 2023 to 30~September 2026, eight-hour averages with each exchange's weights reproduce the settlements that identify the premium to a median absolute error of $0.03$~bps on Binance ($93\%$ within $0.1$~bps; ETH $0.04$~bps and $87\%$) and $0.003$~bps on Bybit (all within $0.1$~bps for both assets), once Bybit's switch from equal to rising weights on 7~March 2024 is allowed for.

From 11~December 2023 to 30~September 2026 Hyperliquid's BTC premium exceeded Binance's in $80\%$ of hours and Bybit's in $81\%$, by medians of $2.19$ and $2.20$~bps; for ETH the medians are $1.69$ and $1.67$~bps, in $77\%$ of hours (Table~\ref{tab:gap}, Panel~A; Figure~\ref{fig:gap}(a,b)). The quarterly median is positive in all twelve quarters for every pair, and its $95\%$ interval, from a moving-block bootstrap with one-week blocks, excludes zero in every full quarter. The gap is persistent in sign but not in size: for BTC its yearly median was $3.8$~bps in 2024, $3.0$ to $3.1$~bps in 2025 and $0.6$~bps in 2026 (to September); by quarter it fell to $0.3$ to $0.5$~bps in 2026Q1 and 2026Q2 and widened again to $1.3$ to $1.5$~bps in 2026Q3 (ETH $2.4$, above its 2025 median). For BTC, the interval of the monthly median covers zero in April and May 2026 against both exchanges under every block length, and in a few other months that differ with the block length (with one-week blocks, five full months in all against Binance and three against Bybit). Deviations of the daily mean gap from its average halve in under three days ($2.8$ for BTC against Binance; about two days around each quarter's mean), though the autocorrelation at 30~days is still $0.21$: the level itself moves slowly. For ETH the weekly mean gap was negative for four weeks from 31~March 2025 against both exchanges.

The gap is not a constant offset. Over the estimation window the quantiles of Hyperliquid's eight-hour BTC block means lie $0.20$~bps below Binance's at the 5th percentile, $0.73$~bps above at the median and $4.72$~bps above at the 90th: the exchanges' premiums are packed just below the band, Hyperliquid's spread across it, and moving Hyperliquid's block means down by the difference of the two medians still leaves $34.0\%$ of them in the band, against Binance's $5.9\%$ at the floor. Pooled from December 2023, the gap is larger when the exchange's premium lies inside the band (BTC medians of $3.91$ and $3.78$~bps, against $1.69$ and $1.66$ below it), but four in five below-band hours fall in 2025 and 2026, when the gap was smaller, and within 2024 the ordering reverses ($5.05$ and $5.14$~bps below the band against $3.96$ and $3.92$ inside it). The gap does not move with realised volatility (daily rank correlations of $0.05$ to $0.06$ for BTC).

\subsection{What the gap accounts for}

Panel~B of Table~\ref{tab:gap} splits the difference between Hyperliquid's share at the floor and an exchange's into three steps: \emph{location} (Hyperliquid's premium shifted by the quarter's median gap), \emph{averaging} (Hyperliquid's premium averaged over the exchange's eight-hour blocks with the exchange's weights) and \emph{dispersion} (the exchange's premium in place of Hyperliquid's at the same location; Appendix~\ref{app:gap}). The split depends on the order of the three steps, so we report the Shapley value with location's range over the six orders, and group quarters by where the exchange's median hourly premium $\tilde P_X$ lay.

In the eight quarters in which Binance's median premium lay at or below the band's lower edge, Hyperliquid's BTC funding was at the floor in $58.1\%$ of hours against $13.2\%$ of Binance's settlements, and location accounts for $41.6$ of the $44.9$ points ($19.3$ to $65.2$ over the orders), averaging for $2.1$ and dispersion for $1.2$. Of these, in the five from 2024Q3 to 2025Q4 (all but 2024Q4, when Binance's median premium lay inside the band) location accounts for about all of the difference, $51.8$ of $50.0$ points ($42.3$ of $40.9$ with 2024Q4), but in the three of 2026 for $24.5$ of $36.2$, and for less than half in 2026Q1 and 2026Q2 (Table~\ref{tab:gap-quarters}), against $55\%$ to $70\%$ for ETH. In those two quarters the gap was $0.3$ to $0.5$~bps and the median premiums of all three venues lay below the band, so that the dispersion step, how much of each premium's distribution reached into the band, carried more; in 2026Q3 location again accounts for about all of the difference ($51.8$ of $50.2$ points). Figure~\ref{fig:gap}(c) shows a quarter in which location decides.

In the two quarters in which the exchanges' premiums were positive, 2023Q4 (from 11~December) and 2024Q1, the ordering reverses: Hyperliquid's BTC share is $31.7\%$, against $44.9\%$ on Binance and $54.5\%$ on Bybit, with location negative in every order (Figure~\ref{fig:gap}(d)). Quarter by quarter the reversal holds in seven of the eight pair-quarters, BTC against Bybit in 2023Q4 being the exception; with two quarters, one of them partial, we read it as suggestive. Where an exchange's median lay inside the band but at or below zero the shares are closer, $58.3\%$ against $53.0\%$ on Binance and $62.6\%$ against $47.4\%$ on Bybit, but location's part differs: $-0.1$ of $5.3$ points against Binance ($-13.3$ to $13.5$ over the orders), $14.0$ of $15.2$ against Bybit, whose group adds 2025Q3, when its median lay $0.09$~bps inside the band. ETH shows the same reversal and closer shares, but there location exceeds the small difference, $4.0$ of $2.1$ and $11.6$ of $4.1$ points (Table~\ref{tab:gap-eth}).

\subsection{Candidate sources}

Why Hyperliquid's premium sits higher is not identified here. Table~\ref{tab:construction} lists the construction candidates: a median-of-medians oracle against weighted-average indices, a 20{,}000~USDC impact notional, a one-hour equal-weighted average against eight-hour rising weights (the averaging part of the split is small) and quanto margining; a different mix of traders is a further one. Two can be checked with public data. If traders priced Hyperliquid's book in USDC terms, its premium would fall one for one as USDC gains on USDT; the daily gap's slope on the daily mean of $\ln(\text{USDC}/\text{USDT})$ (Binance spot, in bps) is $-0.125$ for BTC against Binance (HAC s.e.\ $0.048$) and $-0.07$ to $-0.02$ for the other pairs, far from $-1$, and the BTC--Binance slope vanishes within quarters ($-0.037$, s.e.\ $0.038$). Hyperliquid's oracle page changed from `spot prices' to `spot mid prices' between 6~October and 10~December 2025 (Table~\ref{tab:construction}, note~b); the BTC gap against Binance averaged $3.2$~bps less in the 28~days from 10~December than in the 28~days to 6~October ($1.3$ to $3.1$~bps less for the other pairs), but among all $905$ placements of two 28-day windows with $64$~days between them, the mean moves at least as much, up or down, in $12\%$ (a fall at least as large in $9\%$; $17\%$ to $62\%$ for the other pairs), so the change can be neither excluded nor identified as a cause. The gap agrees in sign with the funding spread of \citet{lau2026carry}: a higher premium raises funding one for one outside the band and lifts it to the floor inside it.

\subsection{The premium}\label{ssec:msar}

\begin{table}[tbp]
  \centering
  \caption{The two-state \MSAR(1) of equation~\eqref{eq:msar} on the premium, estimation window, with the fitted chain and robust inference. $\hat\mu_k$ is the regime level, not an intercept. Half-life intervals transform $\hat\phi_k$'s Wald interval; an end at $\infty$ means that interval reaches a unit root. $\hat\tau^{\text{sw}}_k$ is the first hour at which the expected deviation from regime~$k$ falls to one half under the fitted chain. Side of unity: the Wald test of $\phi_{\text{calm}} = \phi_{\text{stressed}}$ at $5\%$ (n.s.: not significant); the $95\%$ sets are descriptive.}
  \label{tab:msar}
  \footnotesize
  \input{tables/table3b_funding_robust.tex}
\end{table}

Fitted to Hyperliquid's hourly rate, a two-state Markov-switching AR(1) devotes one regime to the floor, with a variance below $10^{-31}$ in each of four estimations: the censoring makes the likelihood unbounded there. We therefore model Hyperliquid's BTC premium $y_t$ in bps, with the mean-adjusted form of \citet{hamilton1989} in which the autoregressive coefficient and the variance switch as well,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:msar}
  y_t - \mu_{S_t} \;=\; \phi_{S_t}\bigl(y_{t-1} - \mu_{S_{t-1}}\bigr) + \sigma_{S_t}\,\varepsilon_t,\qquad \varepsilon_t \stackrel{\text{iid}}{\sim} \mathcal{N}(0,1),
\end{equation}
where $S_t$ is a two-state Markov chain, estimated by maximum likelihood from $40$ estimations of $40$ random initialisations each (Appendix~\ref{app:estimation}). The within-regime half-life $\tau_k = \ln 0.5/\ln|\phi_k|$ counts the hours in which half of a dislocation decays while the chain stays in regime~$k$.

\begin{figure}[tbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/fig7b_october_hour_robust.pdf}
  \caption{The hour that sets the size of the asymmetry. \textbf{(a)} Hourly premium, 8 to 13~October 2025, against the band. \textbf{(b, c)} $R$ (filled markers) and $\Rsw$ (open diamonds) on the estimation window and its upper-tail variants, on the other windows, and on the later window with one hour set to the October maximum, under the Hessian (b) and the sandwich covariance (c). Whole series: the estimation and later windows together, 2~October 2025 to 30~September 2026 ($T = 8{,}737$). Colour and marker give the Wald test at $5\%$ (blue: $R > 1$; red: $R < 1$; grey: not significant), with its $p$-value at the right; the bars are $95\%$ Fieller sets, descriptive only, and arrows mark sets that run past the axis.}
  \label{fig:october}
\end{figure}

Within regimes the premium halves in $35.6$~hours when calm and $3.2$~hours under stress (Table~\ref{tab:msar}), a ratio $R = \taucalm/\taustr$ of $11.2$. But the stressed regime lasts $2.6$~hours on average, and propagated through the fitted chain a dislocation halves after $16.3$~hours when it arises under stress and $24.5$~hours when it arises in the calm regime, a switching-adjusted ratio $\Rsw$ of $1.51$. A single-regime AR(1) on the same premium gives a half-life of $14.5$~hours; estimates elsewhere are shorter, and for other objects: a median of $3.5$~hours for the basis across 41 dYdX markets \citep{ahmed2026event} and about four hours for Hyperliquid's censored BTC funding rate, two to six hours across BTC, ETH and SOL \citep{le2026funding}.

\begin{figure}[tbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/fig8b_rolling_robust.pdf}
  \caption{Rolling estimates of $R$ (filled markers) and $\Rsw$ (open diamonds) at the window mid-points, windows of $2{,}500$ hours stepped by $450$, under the Hessian (a) and the sandwich covariance (b). Colours, markers and bars as in Figure~\ref{fig:october}.}
  \label{fig:rolling}
\end{figure}

The residuals are far from Gaussian, with an excess kurtosis of $78.9$ (Table~\ref{tab:diagnostics}), so we add the misspecification-robust sandwich covariance \citep{white1982} to the Hessian. Since, with both coefficients positive as in every fit, $R = 1$ exactly when $\phi_{\text{calm}} = \phi_{\text{stressed}}$, a Wald test of that restriction decides the side of unity for $R$ and $\Rsw$ alike (Appendix~\ref{app:estimation}). The asymmetry is significant with the Hessian ($z = 3.71$, $p < 0.001$) but not with the sandwich ($z = 1.01$, $p = 0.31$), and $76.7\%$ of the sandwich variance of $R$ comes from one hour: the record stamped 10~October 2025 23:00~UTC, whose premium fell back to $-3.13$~bps from the series' maximum of $+23.61$ an hour earlier. Both records are genuine: the maximum averages the hour, 21:00 to 22:00~UTC, in which Hyperliquid's auto-deleveraging closed some \$2~billion of positions \citep{chitra2025adl,chitra2026online,campbell2026adl}, and the 23:00 record the hour after it, in a cascade whose effects on the venue's liquidity lasted weeks \citep{lim2026sameshock,lim2026recovery}. The one-hour spike to the maximum is an additive outlier \citep{fox1972}, the kind of observation that distorts estimates of an autoregressive coefficient \citep{denby1979}, and a two-state model can devote its stressed regime to it.

Figures~\ref{fig:october} and~\ref{fig:rolling} measure what the hour carries. Replacing the maximum by the next largest value, $9.52$~bps, lowers $R$ to $3.04$ (Hessian $p = 0.035$, sandwich $p = 0.30$); deleting it leaves $1.83$, significant under neither; winsorising the upper $1\%$, which caps the maximum and $49$ further hours, $40$ of them from October 2025, at $6.75$~bps, leaves $4.49$, significant under both (Table~\ref{tab:sensitivity}). The hour thus sets the size of the asymmetry, and the treatment of October's upper tail decides whether the sandwich supports what remains: capping only October's $41$ upper-tail hours leaves $3.93$ (sandwich $p = 0.036$), while from 11~October on the winsorised series shows no asymmetry ($R = 1.11$; $p = 0.79$ and $0.87$). On the hourly sub-period, which lacks October, $R$ is $0.29$, below unity with the Hessian ($p < 0.001$) but not with the sandwich ($p = 0.093$). On the five months after the window it is $0.23$, below unity under both covariances ($\Rsw = 0.54$); one hour of that later window set to $+23.61$~bps raises it to $4.3$, $11.4$ or $18.0$, depending on where it falls. Of $14$ rolling windows of $2{,}500$ hours (Table~\ref{tab:rolling}), the two earliest lie above unity with the Hessian and none with the sandwich, while six lie below it under both. The second starts on 20~October, after the hour, and still lies above unity with the Hessian ($R = 2.94$, $p = 0.031$), though not with the sandwich ($p = 0.082$). The asymmetry describes the October 2025 episode and its aftermath. A size-of-prize argument, by which dislocations large enough to pay for the offsetting trade are arbitraged faster, would give its sign; \citet{shek2026drag} finds the opposite ordering for extreme funding on Binance, and only several such episodes could tell the two apart.

The likelihood is also multi-modal (Figure~\ref{fig:optima}). Each of our $240$ estimations on the estimation window reaches one of two maxima; the inferior one, $20.14$ log-likelihood points lower, attracts $20$ to $30$ of every $40$ estimations and implies $R = 24.6$. A Markov-switching result resting on a nonlinear function of $\hat\phi$ therefore needs a recorded multi-start protocol, a reported terminal log-likelihood and a test rather than an interval: over the $24$ fits of Tables~\ref{tab:rolling} and~\ref{tab:sensitivity} and both covariances, the log-scale delta interval for $R$ contradicts the Wald test in $6$ of $48$ cases, the linear one in $8$ and Fieller's set in $1$.

A three-state Gaussian hidden Markov model on hourly BTC log-returns, with states ordered by volatility (Appendix~\ref{app:estimation}), separates the estimation window into $14.6\%$, $33.3\%$ and $88.4\%$ annualised volatility, with stationary masses of $20\%$, $60\%$ and $20\%$ and expected durations of $13.1$, $8.8$ and $3.7$~hours (Table~\ref{tab:hmm}): a classifier of hourly conditional volatility, not of market phases, in which no drift separates from zero. This fit stopped at $150$ iterations and cannot be refitted, since its hourly prices before 3~December 2025 are no longer served. On the sub-period, where every fit converges, stopping at $150$ iterations would change no annualised volatility by more than $0.04$ percentage points. The converged fit gives $12.8\%$, $31.0\%$ and $87.6\%$, and clipping the extreme $0.1\%$ of returns, four hours, lowers the high state to $85.4\%$ (Table~\ref{tab:robust}). Two-hourly candles over the whole estimation window give $11.0\%$, $31.7\%$ and $88.9\%$, with a high-to-low cell of $1.1\%$ per two-hour bar, as expected when a bar can contain a passage through the moderate state (the reconstructed hourly chain of Figure~\ref{fig:transitions} gives $0.7\%$ over two hours); the zero of the next paragraph is an hourly statement.

Exits from the high state route through the moderate one ($\hat A_{32} = 0.27$; Figure~\ref{fig:transitions}); the direct move to the low state, $\hat A_{31}$, was zero to three decimals in the original fit. On the sub-period, where the fit converges, the estimate lies exactly on the boundary, and the profile likelihood bounds $A_{31}$ below $0.64\%$ per hour (one-sided $95\%$; $0.92\%$ two-sided), a bound that is conservative within the model ($1.0\%$ rejections at a nominal $5\%$). A zero estimate does not show the transition to be rare: with $A_{31} = 0.3\%$ per hour in the estimation-window chain, $39\%$ of $1{,}000$ simulated samples still return exactly zero, and $19\%$ at $1\%$. The zero is what three Gaussian states make of persistent volatility: fitted to $60$ paths of the Student-$t$ GARCH(1,1) of Table~\ref{tab:benchmarks}, a single-regime model, the three-state model returns $\hat A_{31} = 0$ on every one, and on $53.8\%$ of Gaussian-GARCH paths at the best of $32$ starts per path ($40.4\%$ at the eight starts). Volatility steps down through the intermediate level, consistent with volatility clustering \citep{cont2005volatility}, not with a barrier specific to this venue.

The number of states is descriptive. On the sub-period, with every fit converged from about $200$ starts per $K$, BIC prefers $K{=}4$ to $K{=}3$ by $23.6$ points, while $K{=}3$ gains $176.6$ on $K{=}2$; under Gaussian emissions that the diagnostics reject, BIC counts the components a heavy-tailed, clustered distribution needs, not regimes. The four-state kernel has no direct exit from the top state to the bottom either, and its fourth level has no separate economic reading, the ground on which \citet{ang2012} advise choosing the number of regimes; we keep $K{=}3$, as in the estimation-window fit. A Student-$t$ GARCH(1,1) beats the three-state model on BIC with five parameters against fourteen (Table~\ref{tab:benchmarks}), so the hidden Markov model is a descriptive device. Figures~\ref{fig:dist} and~\ref{fig:dashboard} show the sub-period fit.

\section{Implications and limitations}\label{sec:implications}

Headline funding is a censored measurement of the premium, and how often it is censored depends on the venue and the market phase. Funding compared across venues, whether as a gauge of positioning or of integration \citep{zhivkov2026funding,zhivkov2026temporal}, sets a series censored about half the time against series whose censored share depends on the phase: $6\%$ to $8\%$ for BTC over the estimation window, about a quarter since December 2023, and in the first half of 2024 about as high as Hyperliquid's or higher (Table~\ref{tab:history}). The premiums, which the rule does not censor, are the better basis, read with their constructions in view (Table~\ref{tab:construction}). Funding-arbitrage studies rightly use the cash flow \citep{werapun2026funding,lau2026carry}, which the censoring does not distort. For position sizing, the filtered state probabilities give one-step conditional moments that a mean-variance rule $w_t = \E[r_{t+1}\given\Filt_t]/(\lambda \Var(r_{t+1}\given\Filt_t))$ would turn into time-varying positions. No state's drift separates from zero (Table~\ref{tab:hmm}), and drifts set to zero would give no position; a common non-zero drift $\mu$, in effect a fixed directional view, gives $w_t = \mu/(\lambda \sum_k \Prob(S_{t+1}{=}k\given\Filt_t)\,\sigma_k^2)$, which scales that view by the inverse of the one-step conditional variance: volatility timing, not a volatility target. For execution, the stressed premium regime lasts $2.6$~hours on average, and allowing for switching a dislocation halves in $16$ to $25$~hours whichever regime it starts in; whether a schedule could exploit that is a backtest's question we do not answer.

Every result is in-sample and descriptive: posteriors use parameters estimated on the window they describe, and we run no walk-forward, backtest or transaction-cost exercise. The censoring ordering reverses in the two quarters in which the exchanges' premiums were positive, one of them partial, and otherwise only in isolated months (Hyperliquid below both exchanges in November 2024 and, for BTC, July 2025) and against Binance over 2024Q4; these are too few to characterise when it reverses. The premium gap is a difference between venue-specific premium measures, each taken against the venue's own index or oracle with its own impact size and weights, and the candidate sources cannot be separated with public data; Bybit's index weights are snapshots, and its impact notional is not given in the pages we used. The \MSAR\ is misspecified: the premium index is a within-hour average, which induces a moving-average component, and the residuals are far from Gaussian; the sandwich covariance repairs its standard errors only in part, because one hour dominates it. The return model's Gaussian emission is rejected, a Student-$t$ GARCH fits better on BIC, and the estimation-window fit stopped at $150$ iterations and cannot be re-estimated; its sub-period counterpart is converged. The documentation behind Table~\ref{tab:construction} is as retrieved on 8~October 2026 and need not describe the whole sample.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

One funding rule, common to Hyperliquid, Binance and Bybit, pins the rate to the interest rate whenever the averaged premium lies in a band 10~bps wide. Measured exactly, Hyperliquid's BTC funding sat there in $46\%$ of hours over the estimation window and $55\%$ since December 2023, against $6\%$ to $8\%$ and $24\%$ to $27\%$ of the exchanges' settlements, and ETH is similar. The difference is a difference in premiums: Hyperliquid's sat a median $2.2$~bps above the exchanges' for BTC, persistent in sign, smallest in the first half of 2026 and wider again in its third quarter, and in the two quarters in which the exchanges' premiums were positive, one of them partial, the ordering reversed, which we read as suggestive. Cross-venue work that reads funding as positioning or integration should compare premiums, with their constructions in view; why Hyperliquid's premium sits higher is the question the measurement opens.

The regime models describe one asset, one venue and one episode. The premium's faster reversion under stress belongs to the October 2025 episode and its aftermath: on the estimation window it is significant with the Hessian but not with the sandwich covariance, and one hour of the 10~October crash sets most of its size, the ratio falling from $11.2$ to $1.8$ without that hour and to $3.0$ with the hour set to the next largest value. With the premium's upper $1\%$ winsorised it is significant under both covariances (sandwich $p = 0.033$), and the first rolling window to start after the crash, on 20~October, still finds it with the Hessian ($p = 0.031$); later data reverse it, and its likelihood has a frequent optimum that is not its maximum. The return model's three volatility states and its high-to-low transition at zero discretise clustered volatility in a way a single-regime GARCH reproduces. Natural extensions are a Student-$t$ or generalised-hyperbolic emission \citep{foroni2024hidden}, within-state volatility dynamics \citep{haas2004new}, time-varying transition probabilities \citep{filardo1994business}, more venues, assets and phases, and a carry backtest once the asymmetry has been seen across several episodes.

\section{Estimation details}\label{app:estimation}
\renewcommand{\theequation}{A\arabic{equation}}
\setcounter{equation}{0}

\paragraph{The return model.} With $S_t\in\{1,\dots,K\}$ the latent state and Gaussian emissions, the filtered posterior $\Prob(S_t=k\given\Filt_t)$ uses only the past; the smoothed posterior conditions on the whole sample and is an in-sample diagnostic only. Baum--Welch runs in log space. Every random start (eight per fit; on the data seeded $42 + K$, that is $45$ for $K = 3$ and $44$ for $K = 2$, and on simulated paths one seed per path) runs until the log-likelihood gains less than $10^{-6}$; the first start with the largest final log-likelihood is continued until it changes by less than $10^{-9}$ and no parameter moves by more than $10^{-10}$. For the comparison of $K = 2$ to $5$ each fit starts from about $200$ points: $40$ random starts drawn like the eight (seed $45 + K$), $160$ with state volatilities spread log-uniformly over $0.15$ to $3.5$ times the sample standard deviation (seed $20261009 + K$), and splits of each state of the best fit with one state fewer. The archived estimation-window fit used the eight starts but stopped at $150$ iterations. Standard errors come from a parametric bootstrap of $500$ series simulated from the fitted chain. States are ordered by $\hat\sigma_k$: ordered by $\hat\mu_k$ \citep{stephens2000label}, the labels would switch in $40.2\%$ of bootstrap replications, because two of the three means lie within $0.27$~bps per hour of each other, and the $95\%$ interval for the state with $\hat\sigma = 15.6$~bps per hour would be $[14.6, 90.7]$ instead of $[14.5, 16.5]$.

\paragraph{The premium model.} In~\eqref{eq:msar} $\mu_k$ is the regime level; the fixed-coefficient form would carry the intercept $\mu_k(1-\phi_k)$, which is badly behaved with $\hat\phi_k$ near unity. We estimate with \texttt{statsmodels}, which combines an EM initialisation, a random start search and quasi-Newton optimisation with the Hamilton filter and the smoother of \citet{kim1994dynamic}, from $40$ estimations (seeds $20260802$ to $20260841$) of $40$ random initialisations each, and report the highest terminal log-likelihood. An estimation whose start search fails numerically is dropped, and a start whose log-likelihood is not finite, whose smallest regime variance falls below $10^{-6}$ times the sample variance or whose Hessian is singular cannot be the reported optimum.

\paragraph{Half-lives under switching.} With $P$ the transition matrix and $\Phi = \operatorname{diag}(\phi_1, \phi_2)$, the expected deviation $x_t = y_t - \mu_{S_t}$ after a unit dislocation in regime $k$ is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:switching}
  m_k(h) = \E[x_{t+h}\given x_t = 1, S_t = k] = e_k^{\top} (P\Phi)^h \mathbf{1},
\end{equation}
and $\tau^{\text{sw}}_k$ is the first hour with $m_k(h) \le 1/2$, interpolated log-linearly between whole hours. The ratio $\Rsw$ of the calm to the stressed $\tau^{\text{sw}}_k$ exceeds unity exactly when $\phi_{\text{calm}} > \phi_{\text{stressed}}$, provided both $\phi_k$ are positive and $p_{cc} + p_{ss} - 1$, the second eigenvalue of $P$, is positive; it therefore takes the Wald verdict whenever both coefficients are positive and this eigenvalue is significantly positive, as they are in every fit we report. Its simulated interval draws $50{,}000$ parameter vectors from their asymptotic normal distribution, the transition probabilities on the logit scale.

\begin{sloppypar}
\paragraph{Covariances and intervals.} The Hessian covariance is $-H^{-1}$, the outer product of gradients is $(S^{\top}S)^{-1}$, with $S$ the matrix of per-hour scores, and the sandwich is $H^{-1}S^{\top}S H^{-1}$ \citep{white1982}. The side of unity of $R$ is the two-sided Wald test of $\phi_{\text{calm}} = \phi_{\text{stressed}}$ at $5\%$. The linear and log-scale delta intervals and Fieller's set \citep{fieller1954} are descriptive; fed with delta-method variances of $\ln|\phi_k|$, Fieller's set is first-order here too, and it can be unbounded.
\end{sloppypar}

\section{The premium gap: construction and validation}\label{app:gap}

\paragraph{Alignment and settlements.} Hyperliquid stamps each funding record at the end of the hour whose premium it averages, so the record stamped $t$ covers $[t-1\text{h}, t)$; for the same hour we average the closes of the exchange's one-minute premium-index klines that open in $[t-1\text{h}, t)$ and keep an hour only when all $60$ minutes are present. An hour enters $D_t$ only when both venues have it: one Binance hour lacks minutes, which leaves $24{,}576$ hours against Binance and $24{,}577$ against Bybit. An exchange settlement at $s$ averages its premium over $(s-8\text{h}, s]$: Binance weights its five-second samples $1, \dots, 5{,}760$, which we rebuild from minute closes with linear weights, and Bybit weights its minute samples $1, \dots, 480$ from the settlement of 7~March 2024 16:00~UTC and equally before.

\paragraph{Decomposition.} For a group of quarters let $G$ be each quarter's median hourly gap. Three steps take Hyperliquid's share of hours in the band to the exchange's share of settlements at the floor: \emph{location} shifts the premium by $-G$; \emph{averaging} replaces hours by averages over the exchange's eight-hour blocks, weighted as the exchange weights them; \emph{dispersion} replaces Hyperliquid's premium by the exchange's at the same location, shifted by $+G$ while the location is still Hyperliquid's. Each step is taken (1) or not (0), and each of the eight combinations (location, averaging, dispersion) gives a share in the band, lettered as in the replication package: (a) $(0,0,0)$, Hyperliquid's hours; (b) $(1,0,0)$, the same hours shifted by $-G$; (c) $(0,1,0)$, Hyperliquid's eight-hour averages; (d) $(1,1,1)$, the exchange's settlements at the floor; (e) $(1,1,0)$, the averages of (c) shifted by $-G$; (f) $(0,0,1)$, the exchange's hours shifted by $+G$; (g) $(1,0,1)$, the exchange's hours; and (h) $(0,1,1)$, the exchange's eight-hour averages, rebuilt from its minute klines, shifted by $+G$. Location thus moves a$\to$b, c$\to$e, f$\to$g and h$\to$d; averaging a$\to$c, b$\to$e, f$\to$h and g$\to$d; dispersion a$\to$f, b$\to$g, c$\to$h and e$\to$d. In a given order of the three steps, a step's effect is the fall in the share when it is taken; its Shapley value averages that effect over the six orders, and the three values add up to (a) minus (d). Tables~\ref{tab:gap} and~\ref{tab:gap-eth} also give location's smallest and largest effect over the orders, and Table~\ref{tab:gap-quarters} dispersion's as well. Only (d) uses the settlements themselves; their small difference from the rebuilt averages enters each step's Shapley value equally.

\paragraph{Intervals.} The $95\%$ intervals of the monthly and quarterly medians of $D_t$ are percentile intervals from a moving-block bootstrap with $168$-hour blocks and $2{,}000$ replicates; $24$- and $72$-hour blocks, run from the same seed, serve as a sensitivity check. The partial quarter 2023Q4 and month December 2023 are reported apart.
